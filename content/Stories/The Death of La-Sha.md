> [!Note] A Note on Spellings
> There is a slight difference in the pronunciation of 'Sh' in Tuklut versus the similar Ruchun-derived Siychur. As a result, many Tuklut texts refer to the historical figure La-Kha as La-Sha. They are the same person.

## Foreword to Book I

In this passage, widely believed to be chronologically the first of the poem, we are introduced to La-Sha, Mak-Lamra (high king) of the Tuklua people, and the head of the Kama clan. She is retiring in her feasting hall in the mountains having completed another successful season of raiding in the south lands, and many of her warriors and advisors are with her. She proclaims through her kin-warden Apt that she wishes to have a ‘practice of arms’, a mock combat, tournament and athletic competition the Tuklua have practised for generations. She is asked by Paal, a warrior, who is noticeably speaking out of turn, what the prize is of this. Paal is a masculine warrior, not a feminine lord, and thus ought to have ceded the right to speak to his kin-master. He gets away with it presumably because he is either known for direct counsel, by virtue of his feats of arms, or perhaps because he is an old soldier and owed respect above his station (he appears many times throughout the Death, often interjecting to ask a question the audience might be asking). 

La-Sha is visited by Jama for the first time in this passage, who uses a childhood memory to impart a lesson via her old parent Kito; act while you are young, and do not get complacent. It is a common tactic in Tuklut poems to disguise the gods as omens, memories and flashes of inspiration. They also frequently impersonate others, so as not to shock mortal minds. Jama here reminds La-Sha of her own ‘Lesson of the Stone’, which is still taught by mentors in circles around fire pits in Tuklua. She then says that the winner of the mock combat will be casted (made a lord) and be the most exalted among all her followers, a great prize indeed.

The Uswa then turn to feasting, and here we see an insight into the Tuklua’s customs of the right to speak, precedence and boasting. First, they fill a basin with wine plundered and harvested, with the best contributor noted for their success and generosity. Then Apt prepares the finest cuts of meat and sets them to roast while the assembled warrior retinue of La-Sha drinks. The lords do not fetch the wine themselves, but send feminine cupbearers to bring them the wine. One of these cupbearers, Tji, is described in detail, and her master Hin-Amba praises her for her virtues. This begins the habit of boasting, and also serves as an unusual exception to precedence at courtly functions such as these. Hin-Amba speaks herself, not through a herald, indicating she is not making a formal proclamation but is speaking casually. It is additionally significant because there is no note of La-Sha being served before anyone else, which is an unusual omission by the standards of modern Tuklua who place great importance on the best food being given to the highest in status. In La-Sha’s military court, she seems far more lax on such matters.

Once this is done and the meat is ready to eat, the kin are served as family groups in silver trenchers. Multiple lords speak of the accomplishments of their warriors; Hat-Raun boasts of her soldier Tan’s great appetite for war and huge size; Lin-Kao of her child Rehan’s ability to ascertain the value of spoils of war; and Do-Rhaum boasts of Atl’s spear throwing and Dau’s playing of the carnyx and his skill at wrestling.

La-Sha takes no part in this, for it is assumed everyone there already knows she has nothing more to prove. She retires, quite drunk, and is undressed by her kin-maids Vis, Tid and Aura, the last of whom is noted for being a Prakanch ‘bride’ and the most favoured of her kin. She takes La-Sha’s cloak to wash, but La-Sha forbids this, saying she wants her people to know she too walks in the dirt with them.

She is noted to ‘lay with her kin’, which indicates here the fluid nature of Uswa relations. Notably, Apt is absent from the bed, for his advanced age and role as herald surpasses any expectation to bear a child. Instead, she lays with her handmaidens and with Apt’s student Kus, who was once a soldier of La-Sha who was wounded in the foot, and now seeks to bear her children. Here we get a sense of the importance of casting and uncasting, and in which context it is honourable and which it is not. As Apt vouches for Kus, and he proved himself strong in combat, he was invited to the La kinship and deemed worthy of bearing the heir to the Mak-Lamra, a rare honour.

La-Sha then falls asleep, and Jama speaks to her again, this time as her old comrade and mentor Ahl-So who fought with her (and was slain during) the civil war that crowned La-Sha as Mak-Lamra of the clans. Jama, disguised, tells her not to get complacent, to remember the disaster of the eruption of Amsyr that sent her ancestors fleeing, and to heed both the warnings of the gods and her own ambition to conquer more civilised domains. The book then ends.

Of note here is the use of the cognomen, for only the lords of a kinship is permitted to use it. La-Sha is ‘La’ because she is the head of the family. Even in the looser social context of La-Sha’s hall, it is still enforced who speaks for whom and when. Soldiers do not brag of their own exploits, this is left to their kin-matrons. Equally, we see the communal nature of Uswan familial structures come out: while the Tuklut prize martial glory above all, plenty of mention is given to Apt, who is a bodyguard and herald to La-Sha; to Aura, her chief handmaiden; and particularly to Tji the cupbearer of Hin-Ambra who is specifically described as being the subject of envy for other guests at the feast. In all cases, the description and action of their masters is not diminutive, if anything they appear exceptionally proud of their domestic servants, if in a possessive sense for some.
## I - La-Sha Stirs the Appetite of the Kama

In Lamranat sits fearsome Lha-Sha, most revered Mak-Lamra of the Tuklut. She watches the feasting in her hall in the harvest season, and the harvest has been rich. She calls to her herald, the wide-ribbed Apt, who attends her. “Speak to the Kama my words, chested Apt, I have something to say. Speak to them that the harvest is rich this season, and the spoils are plentiful, and the reaping of the bounty is great. Speak that it is in my mind, by mine own artifice or the Ancestors who grace us always, that I intend a practice of arms in my hall, and that all who attend will make glory of themselves in the witness of Jama and the Twelve.”

And Apt did not slow to speak. He cracked his mighty staff, the brass reforged twelve times from his beating arms, and the shuddering of Kama hall was silent until he spoke. “The words of your Mak-Lamra I speak; the harvest is rich this season, and the spoils are plentiful, and the reaping of the far-bounty is great. It is in her mind to intend a practice of arms in this hall, and all who attend will make glory of themselves in the witness of the Jama and the Twelve.”

So he spoke with the words of the Lamra. A stir was put in the hearts of the feasters, as they were blooded Tuklua who sail light down the river and heavy up it. Paal, kinchild of the haughty Thra, from who’s hand the salt weed of the south flows fast, was the first to quell the clamour with his hands raised in command. “What is the victor’s prize? No one among us fights for nothing, for it would be as to plough a barren field, or take a sling to a starving bird. It is meaningless to fight without a prize, it is the undoing of a fighter, unwise and not bold. When our clans were lesser, and no great Lamra was our head, we fought for only pride and honour, and were the lesser for it. Jama’s wisdom taught us to fight only when there is gain, and so I ask of she who speaks through your voice, wise Apt, what is the prize on offer?”

And Apt turned to La-Sha, and the hall followed him. Upon her dias she thought to herself, for the riches of this harvest were truly wonderful, and what more could she promise her brave and skilful soldiers than they had already taken? Jama put the thought in her mind, for he foresaw the wickedness of indecision and did not want for her to waste the moment. In the guise of her kin-rearer Kito, cunning Jama put a memory in her mind. La-Sha was a child again, uncasted and throwing stones as children do at a tree upon a peak beyond a valley. Old Kito came to them and asked of them a question. “You throw the stone at the distant tree, but your arm is not enough to strike it. Why do you throw the stone?”

And the memory of the child Sha answered “One day my arm will be strong enough to hit the tree with the stone. I will grow if I practice and I will hit the tree with my own strength.”

Kito said “This is one way. It is a long way, and depends on many things. You must throw the stones every day for three passings of the winter to be strong enough, and then you will not have the length of arm. So you must wait three more passings to have the reach, and then may lack the height. So you must wait three more passings to stand tall. And then, you must find more stones, for you will have exhausted the bosom of the mountain in your quest, and three more passings might exceed you before this is done. In twelve passings you will hit the tree, yes, but do you wish to wait?”

Little Sha would say “That is the way of things. The tree waits and it is stronger for the waiting, and its fruits are sweeter, and the trunk stouter, and the sap thicker, and the roots more buried in the ground to weather the storm and the sliding land.” So old Kito would reply “And what of the tree that is felled for the winter-fire? And what of the elder tree who is humbled by the axe, whose ancient years are nothing to the flint and the brass? No one ever felled a sapling: it is too thin to be worth as a fire’s food, and too important. Yes, they grow, but it is how they grow that matters most.”

Sha asked “Then what am I to do?”. And wise Kito said “Think of the lessons of Jama taught to the Twelve. Did Jama say ‘Wait and grow old and the answers will come to you’? No, Jama said that in youth is the time to strike, and the better for it. Only fools fight as elders, their bones are brittle and their spines are worn with the rout. For while you grow stronger now, there will come a day when you curse age for what it takes from you and are blind to what it gives.”

Sha asked “Then what am I to do?” And wise Kito said “You must exercise your mind’s resource. Look at the stones you throw: are they weighted for your hand? Look at your arm: would not a sling extend it? Look to your stance: were you closer to the tree, would not it be larger in your eye and all the easier to strike? These are the lessons of Jama. Do not assume victory will come because it has come before, or because you expect it to come. You must make the victory by your intuition, and those who tower over you will be humbled by your cutting wit.”

So La-Sha woke from her revelry and the idea was in her mind. She said “The prize shall be a carnyx wrought of Ruchan brass, with silver stylings upon its flanks, and its maw is the shape of the stinging asp. And this comes with an omen; they who take this prize shall be glorified above all and win even greater spoils in the passings to come that this year hence. I shall have the winner casted myself as an exalted leader, and they shall make much of the riches of  foreign lands. Such is the reward for the canniest warrior among us. But now only dream of this prize, for it is not among us now. Eat of the food and drink of the wine and take pleasure in the company of kin and clan.”

So spoke the Mak-Lamra, and the people ate of the harvest. First they set a great wide-brimmed basin upon the tripod, and each of the gloried leaders of the raids bid their kin to bring up a measure of the water of the vine. The most was given by At-Pech of the feathered headdress, who’s measure when weighed was eleven skins of the one hundred and nineteen poured.

Next, they had the kin feed the great fire with warm salts and smoked spices, so that the fire’s breath was deep and heady. Then they took the great brass arches and arose them above the feasting fire, and from them they dangled pots overflowing with gravy that spat as it spilled into the flame. Then they embedded blackened stakes around the fire, to cage its anger and bear their meats upon their spits. Apt of the wide-ribs took the fattest of the wild game, a roaming land bird rich in meat white and dark, and let its neck be snapped by his kin. Then he sunk the brass into its skin and pulled from it the tender parts good for eating. These he gave to his kin to spear upon the stakes with pinions. The offal of the bird he did not waste but gave to the fire, for the labours of the Twelve brought hunger upon them so deep that even dead they are not sated. And Jama’s gifts to the Tuklua are such that he can eat even the offal of the wild game, and he treasures the sacrifice. So Apt made sacrifice to the ancients.

While the meat was roasting the clan of Kama made their nest around the wine basin. With heavy hands the cupbearers hurried forth to draw from the wine well a deep cup for their masters. Among them was Tji of the clever hands, whose artistry at weaving was unmatched among the Riwil, who are themselves the greatest by mean of the Tuklut at that craft. And she with her wise eyes waited until the others had drawn their fill of the spiced wine and brought forth the cup of her master, the strong Hin-Amba of Ys’ line, and she drew deep from the basin with her arm to the elbow in the wine. In this way she pulled the richest measure from the basin, where the heavy talent of the drink pools at the depths and into which sinks the best of the yield. She then tips from the cup a mouthful of the water of the vine, so as not to spill more as she brings it to her master, but not enough that another might call her stingy. With her practiced hands she drapes a dark woven cloth over her arm to soak from it the red wine, and transfers the cup to the other so it is warm and firm to the grip. All watched in awe of her as she brings back the cup to Hin-Amba, and when she drinks of it she is roused to speak.

Hin-Amba said to the feasters “Let the meat blacken, and the grain spoil, and the sky turn white with storm cloud, and I will not be unhappy, for this is the sweetest of the wine at this hall, and mine is the sweetest companionship. War is fought for less, but never more, for it while the warrior may bring kin with them in their heart when they go to the distant lands they hasten to return to those who they cherish so. It is the warrior who brings the wine, but it is the kin who make it sweet.”

So spoke Hin-Amba of Ys’ line. And her speaking made envy in the hearts of those who listened, for her kin truly were the most lovely and the most loyal of any present.

Then the meat was declared done by the tenders, and from the fire with tongs was it served onto long silver trenchers. The Kama ate their fill, none more so than Tan who’s portion was twice and half again greater then the next. Tan did not speak, for his master Hat-Raun spoke for him: “Mighty gathered are we of the Kama, for our ships are laden with the taken spoils of the sedentary people of the south. None have taken more than Tan, my kin, for his shoulders are the broader for his victories. It was once the custom of the Hat kin to fast before an undertaking, to consume only the fibres of the reeds and drink only the water of the spring, so that the clamour of battle would be less in the face of the rejoicing of the victory. Tan is not of my blood, so he did not share this custom before I invited him to my hearth. Yet when he fights, the night before he is of full belly and great spirit. He wakes already having taken his share, and takes yet more without being bidden, such is his appetite and his desire.”

In response stands Lin-Kao the vaulter of rivers, and she speaks “Your warrior may be strong, and his courage may be matchless, but let me tell you of another whom might prove his equal. I am still vital of the bone and sharp of the mind enough to say this, though the warrior of which I speak is not only of my kin but of my blood, and more than this, they are of my siring. He is a warrior of unsurpassed greatness in the art of the sacking, for wide is his gaze and great is his judgement of the wealth. He can tell at a glance whether to strip ten lesser warriors of their noble brass or one great warrior of theirs, and every time his decision is to my profit. He has won for me his weight twelve times over in brass, and in cloth, and in fair-won glory. At my side he is always, bearing the crook and the knife at hand, and he has learned well of his sire’s skill in the leaping of walls to reach the soft-skinned tribute within. I say that Tan may be the largest of the warriors, and may carry much upon his beaten back, but none can be said to be greater in sum than my own child Rehan.”

At Lin-Kao’s speech stood Do-Rhum, who’s equal in the spear has not been found. Do-Rhum stands slowly, as is her way, for her killing reach is known to any who cast the spear in anger or for profit. She speaks to the boasters “I cannot doubt that your words are true, Lin-Kao who leaps the rivers, but still you are a parent, and a parent loves their child above all else, and sees no fault in them. And Hat-Raun, your kin is the better for your ferocity, but it is not our way to fight only with our words. Let both of your warriors be tested against mine, who I have taken as apprentice in the throwing of the spear. Atl is the fairer in the cast, his feet are surer than the ranging game upon the mountain, clinging to the lichenous rock. You have all born witness to the cast that ended stout Uqen Azun, of Ijkit’s line, when he held the gates of Aukis against our hard-fought advance across the river ways of the Great Plateau. Azun was the most fearsome of Aukis’ defenders, a warrior of certain surety that his death would not come at the hands of a Tuklut. He raged this and that with his close kin, the stone-skinned Azunir, until Atl put the brass in the nape of his neck where his skin was thin and bared his throat to the sky. Then the battle was won and we all partook in the bounty of Aukis. We owe this all to Atl. And not his lesser is Dau, who is like a child to me, and bears the carnyx without pausing for breath at my side when the battle is at its thickest. He is a great winner at the wrestling as well, unbested in the sand, for his toes can find purchase wet or dry, and when he locked shoulders with the Imagan Kuos in the games he put that famous fighter to shame.”

And so boasted the kin-masters of their warriors, and much joy was had in the comparison of one fighter to another. And they slept in watches of eight, and over them four watched and made merry.

And La-Sha took to the warmth of her hut and was attended by the counsel of her kin. She was heady with the water of the vine, but not so that she could not see a flowing dream. She came into her hut and was undressed of her great cloak by the sober Vis and Tid and Aura, the last of whom a Prakan bride of noble stature. She attended La-Sha when last she was fighting for the masters of that great city, and her skill as the cupbearer was second only to her skill at the dressing and undressing of the mantled king. And so now she unclasps the brass broach from mighty La-Sha’s shoulders, and unwraps the cloth from her waist. She takes the cloak, one so long with victories it trails behind its wearer like a flowing river, and carefully folds it such that the grit of the earth does not stain the rest of the fabric. Then she calls to the servants to have the cloak washed in the clear spring water of the valley. La-Sha speaks “Do not wash my well-worn cloak in the water of the valley. I walked among those who sacked Aukis, and Vetu, and white-stoned Sugal, beloved of the Kiwans, and though I did this at the pay of a foreign master it is no less the accomplishment for it. Let the people see that I tread the same dirt as they, and have bled the same blood as they, exalted as I am now I was not always, and am the better for it.”

So Aura placed the cloak upon the wall, on a fine-hewn peg, and unsandled her master, and ungirdled her, and commanded the servants instead to bring two bowls of water, one for washing and one for tea. And in the cookfire she bid Vis to brew the sleeping drought, and bade Tid to wash the feet of La-Sha, and Aura took the Mak circlet from her master and set it upon her possessions.

And then La-Sha lay with her kin, made fresh from their attentions, and made drowsy by the drought. The Mak-Lamra lay Apt’s student, the vital Kus, strong of the thew. And of her many concubines he was the favoured, for he was marked by the rigour of the games and fought her honour thrice before wound took him from the field. Now he serves the Mak-Lamra at her side in the hall instead of bearing shield at frightful war.

And in her sleep she dreamt a scared dream, for Jama spoke through her ears and the Twelve spoke through her bones. Jama came to her in the form of her beloved mentor, the warrior Ahl-So, who had learned her in the ways of conquest. In his voice Jama said “Great La-Kha, you sleep having exalted the exploits of your clan, and rightly so. But you are allowing your mind’s vision to close, and think too little of yourself and your abilities. Already you have United the Tipjat under the Kama, at the killing of many who did not share your vision, and you no longer fight wars for the Ruchans or the Prakani, nor any other master but yourself. You take their spoils and it is a good life for you. You could die now, surrounded by your concubines, in your splenderous hut, with many treasures to your name in the valley of your birth, and it would be enough.”

“But your heart is already beginning to grow tired of your splendour. You boast of the sacking of Aukis, and Vitu, and fair Sugal, and all of these are accomplishments to many. Yet not one of the Tuklut has toppled Prakan, or Oluk, or any of the great cities of the Kiwans that have not fallen to your hand. These are the ripest fruits, these the oldest and stoutest trees. Your axe has never been sharper, but it is beginning to dull! You are feasting upon your riches when you could be carving your name into history, as do the southern people in their metal books. Go then, continue to eat and drink and cavort yourself in a heaven if your own making. No ancestor would refuse you as you are. But if you would make of yourself a name to be fitting of your prowess, you would take these warriors and go further than any Tuklut has gone before.”

“Once, the gods sent their will through the rumbling of the earth and sky. Your ancestors trembled when Amsyr, that great fertile mountain, shook with indignation at the unfaithfulness of the people who reared the tall vines upon its flanks. Yet they did not heed the warning, and many were buried by the ash. Even this was not the end of their suffering. When the people then fled, they brought with them a sickness to those who’s lands they inhabited, and a darkness in the sky followed them all. The chill of the skies fell upon the land, and only you Tipjat survived with honour intact. Such was the voice the gods spoke with then, but now they speak to you with a softer voice, for of all the gods Jama is the most well-versed in manipulations.”

“Heed the call of your ambition, and yearn to quench it in the fire pits of the grand cities of the south.  As does the storm rolling over the dark seas, tossing and churning with feverish fraught, its stomach twisting with the ire. It bursts its anger upon the banks of the sand and the cliff, wearing away the passage of time in an instant, and makes misery for the fisher folk and the salt farmers who dwell there. So too must you fathom your reckoning before your fire is done, and lay low the world beyond your borders that mocks you for a fool.”

Thus spoke the god Jama to La-Sha as she slept the wine-dark sleep.

# II - The Kamak Games 

  

When the morning weeps its light across heaven’s fields, as does the blood of the woodcarver upon the white bark flesh of the young tree, when she has wounded her palm or her thumb or her wrist upon the sharp flint. She has worked tirelessly until now, when her endurance has reached its peak, and now she must leave the work to wrap her injury. Down the stripped wood her blood flows, leaving behind a stain that hues the pale into a somber red, such does this dawn break upon Kama’s hall.

Fresh still from the season of war, it is known to all that the great sleep will soon take and thus, preparations must be swift. No fleet-footed runners are sent, no messenger ships, no wide-calling criers given instruction or signal-fires are lit to summon the clans, for La-Sha intends this games only for her clanfolk. She is woken by her watchful kin, the glorious Vis, who is master in La’s kinship of the sleeping and the awakening. She first dresses, donning herself a waist-low cloak of cream white and a girdle of leather tanned from a dozen wild beasts. She draws the wakeful draught from the simmering pot, mixing it fine with spring water from the high mountain, and makes a basin for the kin to wash. Then she bids her kinship to wake.

When the La kinship has woken, and washed, and been dressed fine for the address of the people, La-Sha asks of Vis "Dearest Vis, you are my dawn and my dusk, and my eyes and my ears to the waking world when I sleep. Tell me, I was visited by a dream last night, a grand dream that portents wonder and terror in equal measure. My breast is swollen with the promise of the future, and yet still your soft eyes and gentle temperament melt even the raging heart of the warrior. Is it the will of the god that you sooth me so, and fill me with thoughts of the hearth when my destiny may yet be set upon war?”

Vis replied with flowing words “My kin, I would not think to interpret the will of Jama, though it is true I have been blessed with prophecy once before. I know this is why you ask me, and not old Apt, who’s far-fetching wisdom is known for being the greatest in our clan, or noble Aura, the best of your kinship. But no god has revealed to me this vision of yours, so speak it, and I will counsel.”

And La-Sha spoke of her vision, and Vis replied “If my tender affections sooth you so, great Mak-Lamra, then I will soon be forgotten when you venture towards this glorious fame. A warrior may keep their dearest kin in their heart on the march, or at sail, or in the camp, and especially upon the homecoming if the campaign is good, or even if it is ill, for they seek the comforts of their hearth. But in the heat of the battle, I am told, no warrior has time to spare for their lovely kin. You are of two kinships, my lord, one of this hall and one of the battlefield, and your mind must be in two places at any time. If love for me could stay the hateful spear, then let it be resident in your mind when the killing foe is upon you. But I am one who would forgive being forgotten for the moment, if it spared you for the future that is our reunion.”

La-Sha was pleased by this, and went to set the preparations for the games. The runners, swift-footed, ate of the root and drank of the vital nectar, and thus bidden made haste to reach the corners of the land. As they ran, the builders cleared the stones from the flattest part of the valley, and raised the larger stones for the basking of the audience. In fourteen days the assembly was complete, the tents clinging to the mountain side, the fires roaring with song. They multiply like the insects of the spring; first the herder sees only a few here and there, making their forays unto the leaves of the foliage, and then in greater and greater numbers until a swarm obscured the vision. So did the distant and near kinships of the Kama convene at La-Sha’s command.

La-Sha convened all the kinships and bade Apt to her side. She spoke to him with glowing words “Apt, most able of my kin, tell the kinships of the Kama that I have had forged the carnyx by the hands of the Ruchan smiths who serve us at my mercy. For twelve nights they laboured, and the prize is grand enough, yet the beauty of this pales in comparison to what I promise. I shall casts the greatest athlete of the day in my kinship. They shall become of La, they shall exalt in the glory that it brings, and bring further glory to the name. Tell them this.”

And Apt did not slow to speak. He cracked his mighty staff, the brass reforged twelve times from his beating arms, and the reverberation of the striking echoed from the valleys until he spoke. “The words of your Mak-Lamra I speak; La-Sha has had forged the carnyx by the hands of Ruchan smiths who serve her at her mercy. For twelve nights they have laboured, and the prize of grand, though it is pale in comparison to what is yet promised. The greatest athlete today shall be casted, and invited to the La kinship, at my master’s hearth and mine, to feast with us, fight with us, and claim the widest share of the spoils. They will bring further glory to the kinship, such is the will of La-Sha.”

There was a great rattling of joy when the herald spoke, and the games would begin with a tally. All the Kama clan arose, in the order of their worth, to announce their name, their boast, and their champions. The herald of each spoke with the voice of the lord, and made much of the warriors who would compete.

The kinship of Joa spoke first, to the ire of nobody, for Joa’s line was the fiercest of its time with many famed fighters, many of them half-cut with the blood of the Uir, famed for the strength of their bones and their untarnishable vigour. There was Joa-Sib, axel-shouldered, and Joa-Tyir of the western skies, and embittered Joa-Kit his brother.

The kinships of Lon, Bil and Ans brought forth champions as a collective, feared as they were for the prodigious spilling of much blood upon the river shore. Of Lon came Sach, and potent Mor, and the redoubtable Kis, skilled in the spear work. Of Bil’s hearth was Dhrot, the foreign warrior who fought with the cestus, and Iv the war-caller. Of Ans only Wut was present, his kin having perished in the raids.**
